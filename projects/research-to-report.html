Research to outreach

Find the story. Find who it matters to.

A workflow that takes PR colleagues from a topic to a researched report and personalised pitches for relevant journalists—ready for review and sending by the team.

A story needs somewhere to go

The team needed both a report worth a newsroom's attention and a reason to put it in front of a particular journalist. That meant connecting the research to the outreach: what was worth investigating, what the data could support and who would find the story relevant.

We agreed on the initiative together, and I built the workflow from scratch with Claude. The aim was to let colleagues run it themselves, without needing to build the analysis tools or manage the steps behind them.

The hardest part was designing that sequence: where to explore, where to go deeper and where to stop for a person's judgement.

From evidence to a relevant pitch

One rule shaped the whole process: calculate the numbers with code, then write around them. Text and charts refer back to the same computed results. A reference to a missing result stops the workflow.

Colleagues choose the angle and findings, review the analysis and approve the draft. The report then guides journalist selection: both their coverage and their published articles inform who is relevant and how to frame a short pitch for each person.

The output is a report, relevant journalists and personalised mini-pitches. The team reviews the outreach and handles sending separately.

Less time, stories that reached the press

The PR team adopted the workflow. Comparing one report prepared the old way with one produced through the full workflow showed a 65% reduction in time spent. That's a comparison of two reports, rather than an average across repeated runs.

The first report was picked up by leading national publications, with an average Domain Rating (DR) of 90.

Under the hood
  1. Find sources. A Python script searches OpenAlex, Semantic Scholar and arXiv through their APIs, collecting paper details, available abstracts and source links. It combines the results, removes duplicates and ranks candidates using search relevance, publication date and citation counts. That shortlist informs the research brief; it isn't a verdict on scientific quality.
  2. Choose what to explore. The brief proposes angles and analyses. An initial scan surfaces findings, then colleagues choose which deserve a deeper investigation.
  3. Calculate. Python scripts and spreadsheet models produce the figures. Named results are saved in numbers.json, with plain-language explanations alongside the spreadsheet calculations.
  4. Write and check. Writing and chart briefs reference those results through placeholders. Validation checks the references before generation; assembly checks that the keys used in text and charts trace back to computed results. Human review comes before the final document.
  5. Prepare outreach. The report's topic and angle guide journalist selection and short, personalised pitches. Coverage areas and published articles inform both the selection and the pitch. This is report-specific preparation, separate from the shared contact database.

Colleagues run the workflow through their own Claude accounts. Traceable figures still need judgement: a calculation can use a weak assumption, and a correct value can be interpreted badly.

Finding its place

The story can take different shapes, but its evidence needs to hold. In the image, fluid layers move around firmer fragments—a way of thinking about creative freedom supported by something you can return to and check.
